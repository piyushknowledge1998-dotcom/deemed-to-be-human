Ask Yourself: What Would a Smart Person Do?

I’ve been thinking lately about what it means to make the right decision.

Not necessarily the perfect decision because perhaps there is no such thing but a decision that takes me somewhere I will not look back upon with regret. A choice that, even if it fails, still makes sense when judged by the person I was and the information I had at that moment.

And somewhere along the way, I started asking myself a rather simple question:

What would a smart person do if they were in my position?

Strangely enough, the answer is almost always slightly better than the one I was originally considering.

Perhaps it is because the question creates distance between me and my immediate impulses. When I ask myself what I should do, the answer is polluted by fear, ego, laziness, insecurity, attachment and sometimes even the need for instant gratification. But when I imagine another person—someone calmer, wiser and more deliberate—standing exactly where I am, the situation suddenly becomes easier to see.

The facts remain the same.

Only the observer changes.

And sometimes, that is enough.

I have realised that human beings are remarkably good emulators. Almost everything we know how to do has, in some form, been observed before. The way we speak, dress, write, argue, work, love, lead or even think has been influenced by people we have watched.

We imitate first and personalise later.

A child learns language by imitation. A musician begins by playing someone else’s compositions. A writer discovers his voice after reading the voices of dozens of others. Even originality, perhaps, is not the absence of imitation but the point at which enough influences collide and rearrange themselves into something that appears uniquely ours.

So why should decision-making be any different?

If someone wiser has already demonstrated a better way of approaching a situation, there is no particular virtue in deliberately beginning from zero. We can borrow the framework, modify it according to our circumstances, and perhaps arrive at a better decision than the one our instincts alone would have produced.

This reminds me of something one of my moderators once told me during an MUN.

He said that if you truly want to understand a subject, imagine that you have a job connected to that subject.

Do not merely study law-imagine you are a judge.

Do not merely study economics—imagine you are responsible for advising a government during a recession.

The moment you adopt such a role, the nature of learning changes stupendously.

Suppose I am studying law as a student. I may be tempted to memorise definitions, cases and provisions because there is an examination waiting for me at the end.

But if I imagine myself as a judge looking at an actual dispute, suddenly memorisation is not enough.

What happened?

What does the law say?

What precedent exists?

What was the intention behind that precedent?

Are there competing rights involved?

What happens if I rule one way?

What precedent might my own judgment create?

A subject that previously consisted of chapters and sections suddenly becomes a living system of consequences.

Perhaps this is why putting ourselves in somebody else’s shoes can be such a powerful intellectual tool. A role creates responsibility, and responsibility creates stakes.

When the stakes feel real, our mind begins to behave differently.

We ask better questions.

We notice details we would otherwise ignore.

We become less satisfied with superficial answers.

In some sense, we temporarily borrow not merely another person’s position, but another person’s standards.

And this idea extends beyond studying.

It can be used to build a life.

People often say, “Be yourself. Don’t change for anyone.”

I understand what they mean. One should not spend life performing endlessly for the approval of others.

But taken too literally, the advice can become strangely limiting.

What if there are parts of myself that I do not particularly admire?

What if I am undisciplined?

What if I communicate poorly?

What if I avoid difficult conversations?

What if I dress in a way that does not reflect how I want to present myself?

What if I lack confidence, patience, physical fitness, knowledge or courage?

Should I preserve all of these simply because they happen to be part of my present self?

I don’t think so.

If there is something about yourself that you genuinely dislike, and if changing it would make you closer to the person you want to become, then change it.

Change the way you speak.

Change the way you dress.

Change the way you carry yourself.

Change the way you respond when somebody disagrees with you.

Change your habits.

Change your environment.

Change the standards you tolerate from yourself.

You are not betraying your identity.
You are constructing it.

Perhaps one of the strangest assumptions we carry is that identity must always come before behaviour. We tell ourselves:

Once I become confident, I will speak confidently.

Once I become disciplined, I will wake up on time.

Once I become a serious writer, I will write every day.

But perhaps the process often works in the opposite direction.

You speak confidently long before you feel confident.

You wake up on time while still considering yourself undisciplined.

You write while still feeling like someone pretending to be a writer.

For a while, there is an uncomfortable gap between who you are and who you are attempting to become.

That gap can feel artificial.

Almost fraudulent.

But perhaps that is simply what growth feels like from the inside.

You borrow an identity before you completely deserve it.

Then you begin acting according to its expectations.

And if you repeat those actions long enough, something interesting happens.

The performance becomes a habit.
The habit becomes character.
And the character eventually becomes you.

There is probably a version of this behind every form of expertise.

Before someone becomes a researcher, they begin asking questions like one.

Before someone becomes a leader, they begin accepting responsibility like one.

Before someone becomes physically strong, they begin training like a strong person would.

Before someone becomes wise, perhaps they simply begin pausing long enough to ask what a wiser person might do.

You do not always need to know exactly who you are.

Sometimes it is enough to know who you would like to become.

Then behave accordingly.

Of course, this does not mean blindly copying successful people. Someone else’s solution belongs to their circumstances, temperament and time. What works for them may fail completely for you.

Emulation should therefore not mean imitation without thought.

It should mean borrowing perspectives.

You take the discipline of one person, the courage of another, the curiosity of another, the composure of someone else—and slowly construct something of your own.

Maybe we are all, to some extent, mosaics of people we once admired.

And perhaps there is nothing wrong with that.

The important question is whether we are choosing our influences deliberately or absorbing them accidentally.

Because either way, something is shaping us.

So whenever I find myself confused now, I try asking a few different questions.

What would a smart person do here?

What would a disciplined person do?

What would someone who respects himself do?

What would someone looking back at this decision ten years from now wish I had done?

The answers are rarely dramatic.

Usually, the smarter option is boring.

Study instead of postponing.

Ask instead of assuming.

Save instead of spending.

Apologise instead of defending your ego.

Leave something that has clearly stopped being good for you.

Stay when leaving would simply be cowardice.

Take the difficult route when the easy one repeatedly leads nowhere.

And perhaps that is the real usefulness of the question.

It does not magically make us smarter.

It simply gives us a momentary opportunity to escape ourselves.

For a few seconds, we step outside our habits, insecurities and excuses and imagine another version of ourselves standing in the same situation.

A calmer version.

A wiser version.

Perhaps even a future version.

Then, for one decision, we act like them.

And maybe becoming better is nothing more mysterious than doing that repeatedly.

After all, the person we eventually become may simply be the person we kept pretending to be—until one day, we no longer had to pretend.

Written by Piyush Yadav
